1. General risk

Digital assets, and memecoins in particular, are speculative and extremely volatile. Prices can move dramatically in either direction, liquidity can disappear, and a position can become impossible to exit at any price. There is no deposit insurance, no central bank backstop, and no guaranteed market for any token.

You are solely responsible for your trading decisions, position sizing, tax obligations, and compliance with the laws that apply to you.

2. Limits of rug filtering

Chainseer is built to filter out almost all rugs, but “almost all” is not “all”. No filter can guarantee that a token is safe. A coin that passes every check can still lose most or all of its value. Bad actors adapt, and they can:

  • spread supply across many fresh wallets to defeat holder-concentration checks;
  • renounce authorities at launch and still drain liquidity or dump a hidden supply later;
  • use a new deployer wallet with no launch history;
  • fake volume, holder counts, or organic activity to look healthy;
  • change tactics faster than any set of rules can be updated.

Chainseer’s security filters reduce some of this risk; they do not eliminate it. The bot only makes the work easier by narrowing a large number of new coins down to a shorter list. It does not replace your own research or judgement.

3. Screening limits

Screening is heuristic. It applies fixed thresholds to contract settings, holder distribution, deployer history, and market data. Thresholds can be too strict, so good coins may be skipped, or too loose, so bad coins may pass. When data is missing, the bot skips the coin rather than guessing.

A high safety score means a coin cleared more checks cleanly. It is not a prediction of price. Historical outcomes are measured after the fact and do not guarantee that the next alert behaves similarly.

4. Data accuracy and latency

Chainseer depends on third-party market data providers and public blockchain sources. That data may be delayed, rate-limited, incomplete, mislabelled, or temporarily unavailable. Alert delivery also depends on enrichment requests, network conditions, and Telegram.

By the time you see an alert, the price may already have moved substantially. Early buyers may be selling into the demand that the alert attracts. Slippage, fees, and priority costs can turn an apparent opportunity into a loss.

5. Smart-contract and token risk

Tokens can contain malicious or poorly written code. Risks include honeypots that block selling, hidden mint functions, freeze authorities, transfer taxes, blacklists, proxy upgrades, and exploitable liquidity pools. Security metrics can be wrong, stale, or bypassed by a determined developer.

Chainseer requires renounced authorities and blocks several known risk patterns by default, but no automated check can prove that a contract is safe. Always verify the contract and the liquidity yourself.

6. Market, liquidity, and exit risk

Thin liquidity means your own order can move the price against you. You may be unable to sell at the quoted price, or at all. Liquidity can be pulled by the developer or by large holders. Trading fees, priority fees, and failed transactions add cost even when the trade does not execute as intended.

Concentration risk is real: a small number of wallets can hold enough supply to collapse the price. Chainseer caps top-10 concentration by default, but that metric can change after the alert.

7. Automation, availability, and delivery risk

The bot may be offline, rate-limited, misconfigured, or affected by bugs. Alerts may be delayed, duplicated, suppressed, or never delivered. The one-alert-per-token default means you will not receive updates for a token that already alerted. Sell-side alerts are disabled by default and, even when enabled, are best-effort warnings — not an exit guarantee.

Do not build a strategy that depends on receiving a message at a precise moment. Use independent risk controls, including position sizing and stop-loss discipline that you control.
